    Clients¶

    A client is an application the provider answers, known by its client_id. It holds the addresses a browser may be sent back to, the one method it authenticates with, the scopes and resources it may ask for, and how it wants the documents written for it. A client comes from one of two places: the configuration of the deployment declares it, or it registered itself at the registration endpoint. Both are read by the same reader under the same rules, so the same members mean the same thing wherever they were written; the source decides what a member left out stands for, who may change the client and how it goes away. A registration is held to two rules more, when it is written: the scopes it names are ones its tenant hands out, and a sector_identifier_uri it names is backed by the document behind it (pairwise subjects).

    Declaring one¶

    Clients a deployment knows in advance are declared under beffroi.clients, keyed by their client_id.

    config/packages/beffroi.yaml
    beffroi:
    clients:
        billing:
            client_name: 'Billing'
            logo_uri: 'https://billing.example.com/logo.svg'
            policy_uri: 'https://billing.example.com/privacy'
            redirect_uris: ['https://billing.example.com/oidc/callback']
            post_logout_redirect_uris: ['https://billing.example.com/']
            token_endpoint_auth_method: private_key_jwt
            jwks_uri: 'https://billing.example.com/.well-known/jwks.json'
            scope: 'openid profile email offline_access invoices.read'
            resources: ['https://api.example.com/']

    The declarations are read as a whole, the first time the provider reads a client, and one that does not hold together is refused there with the client and the rule it broke, before anything is served under it: bin/console beffroi:client:list reads them, and is the quickest way to see a mistake. The same refusal reaches a client that registers itself, as the 400 of RFC 7591 §3.2.2.

    What a member left out means depends on the source. A declaration takes what an application that signs a person in comes for; a client that registers itself takes what RFC 7591 §2 says it gets.

    Member Declared and left out Registered and left out
    grant_types authorization_code and refresh_token authorization_code
    scope openid profile email openid, or the default set the policy of the tenant names
    token_endpoint_auth_method The first method wired, client_secret_basic client_secret_basic
    access_token_format The first shape wired, jwt jwt, a client that registers itself never naming this member
    subject_type The first kind wired, public The same

    A secret is chosen by the deployment for a declared client, and drawn by the provider for a registered one. A declared secret is at least 32 visible ASCII characters (RFC 6749 appendix A.2), a value to generate and put in an environment variable, never in the file. It is hashed with the password hasher of the core the first time the client is looked up in a process, and kept as a hash, except for a client that authenticates with client_secret_jwt: that method computes a MAC with the secret, so the provider keeps the secret itself. A client registers a secret exactly when the method it registered proves one, and a declaration that carries a secret for any other method is refused.

    What a client registers¶

    Every member below is spelled the same in the configuration and in a registration request, under the name RFC 7591 §2, OIDC Registration §2 or the specification of its feature gives it, the members marked deployment being this provider's own. secret is written in the configuration alone, and software_id, software_version and software_statement in a registration request alone: the configuration has no such option and refuses it. A member this provider does not read is refused rather than ignored, and a value of the wrong kind is refused rather than converted: a string where a list is expected, or "true" where a boolean is.

    The members marked deployment are written by whoever declares a client, or by the registration policy of the tenant. They are exceptions to a rule, or privileges, and a client that registers itself never obtains one: one in the body of its registration request is refused with invalid_client_metadata, and a software statement does not grant one either.

    Who is asking¶

    Member What it decides
    client_name The name the consent page shows. A client that registers none is shown under its client_id
    logo_uri The image the consent page shows. The browser of the person loads it from the client, with no referrer, and the provider never fetches it
    policy_uri, tos_uri, client_uri The pages the consent page links to, in that order
    contacts Ways to reach the people responsible for the client. No page shows them

    Where a browser goes¶

    Member What it decides
    redirect_uris Where the answer of the authorization endpoint may go, compared byte for byte with the redirect_uri of a request (RFC 6749 §3.1.2.3)
    post_logout_redirect_uris Where a browser may be sent after a logout, compared the same way
    initiate_login_uri Where the provider sends a browser to have this client begin a login (OIDC Core §4), from /initiate-login?client_id=.... HTTPS, on a loopback host as anywhere else

    What it may ask for¶

    Member What it decides
    grant_types The grants it may use at the token endpoint, among those the provider is wired with
    response_types code, and nothing else. It is not kept, the grants saying the same thing: a registration that names code without the grant that answers with one is refused
    scope The closed list of scopes it may ask for, space delimited (RFC 6749 §3.3). A scope outside it is invalid_scope
    resources Deployment. The resources it may ask an access token for (RFC 8707 §2). See resources
    authorization_details_types The types of authorization detail it may ask for (RFC 9396 §2), among those the provider is wired with. See authorization details

    How it proves who it is¶

    Member What it decides
    token_endpoint_auth_method The one method it authenticates with, among those the provider is wired with. See client authentication
    secret Configuration only: the shared secret of a declared client whose method proves one
    jwks, jwks_uri Its public keys, inline or behind a URL, one or the other and never both. A symmetric key (kty: oct) is refused
    tls_client_auth_subject_dn, tls_client_auth_san_dns, tls_client_auth_san_uri, tls_client_auth_san_ip, tls_client_auth_san_email What names it in its certificate (RFC 8705 §2.1.2), exactly one of the five, for tls_client_auth and for no other method

    How its requests are made¶

    Member What it decides
    requires_pkce Deployment. true by default. false is accepted for a confidential client only, which may then send no code_challenge; a challenge it sends is verified all the same
    require_pushed_authorization_requests Its authorization requests are only read from a pushed request (RFC 9126 §6)
    require_signed_request_object Its authorization requests are only read from a request object, whose signature is what request_object_signing_alg says, and its backchannel authentication requests only from an object it signed
    request_object_signing_alg The one algorithm its request objects are signed with, or none for unsigned objects. Left out, any algorithm the provider verifies, and never an unsigned object
    request_uris The URLs a request object of this client may be read back from, compared byte for byte, fragment included

    What it receives¶

    Member What it decides
    access_token_format Deployment. The shape of its access tokens: jwt or opaque. See access tokens
    require_sender_constrained_tokens Deployment. A token request that proves neither a certificate nor a key is refused with invalid_request rather than answered with bearer tokens
    tls_client_certificate_bound_access_tokens Its tokens are bound to the certificate of its connection whichever way it authenticated (RFC 8705 §3.4), and a token request that presents none is refused with invalid_request
    id_token_signed_response_alg The algorithm its ID tokens and logout tokens are signed with. Left out, RS256, the first algorithm wired
    id_token_encrypted_response_alg, id_token_encrypted_response_enc Its ID tokens encrypted to one of its keys once signed. The two go together, neither has a default
    userinfo_signed_response_alg Its UserInfo answer as a signed JWT (application/jwt). Left out, plain JSON
    userinfo_encrypted_response_alg, userinfo_encrypted_response_enc Its UserInfo answer encrypted to one of its keys
    authorization_signed_response_alg The algorithm of its signed authorization responses. Left out, RS256
    authorization_encrypted_response_alg, authorization_encrypted_response_enc Its signed authorization responses encrypted to one of its keys
    requires_consent Deployment. true by default. false names a first-party application, which no consent page is shown for

    What encryption a client may register, and which of its keys a document is addressed to, is on the page about encryption.

    Who it sees¶

    Member What it decides
    subject_type public or pairwise, the kind of sub it sees for a person (OIDC Core §8)
    sector_identifier_uri The sector its pairwise identifiers belong to. See pairwise subjects

    Logout, backchannel authentication and token exchange¶

    Member What it decides
    backchannel_logout_uri, backchannel_logout_session_required Where a logout token is posted, and whether the client reads its sid (Back-Channel Logout 1.0 §2.2). See logout
    frontchannel_logout_uri, frontchannel_logout_session_required The page loaded in a frame at a logout, and whether it receives iss and sid (Front-Channel Logout 1.0 §2)
    backchannel_token_delivery_mode poll or ping, for a client of the backchannel flow (CIBA Core §4). No default. See backchannel authentication
    backchannel_client_notification_endpoint Where the provider calls a ping client back. Required with ping, refused with another mode or with none
    token_exchange Deployment. For a client of the token exchange grant: subject_token_types, and may_act_for_a_user. See token exchange
    may_act Deployment. Who may act for the subject of a token issued to this client (RFC 8693 §4.4)

    What it says of its software¶

    Member What it decides
    software_id, software_version Registration only: what the software the client is an instance of calls itself, and its version (RFC 7591 §2)
    software_statement Registration only: read by the registration mode that takes one, never as metadata. See dynamic registration

    The addresses a client registers¶

    Every URL a client registers, whatever the provider does with it, is held to one rule: an absolute URI, with no fragment and no credentials, whose host is a name of letters, digits, hyphens and dots or an IP address, in HTTPS. Plain HTTP is accepted on a loopback host only, localhost, a name under .localhost, an address of 127.0.0.0/8 or [::1]. A custom scheme is not an address the provider sends anybody to, and is refused.

    Three members depart from it, each for its own reason. initiate_login_uri is HTTPS on a loopback host too, OIDC Registration §2 giving it no exception. request_uris may carry a fragment, which OIDC Core §6.2 gives a meaning, and the URL is read back without it. resources are identifiers and never fetched, so a URN is a resource as an HTTPS URL is (RFC 8707 §2). tls_client_auth_san_uri is no address either: it is the text a certificate is compared with, and only has to be non-empty.

    A redirect URI matches byte for byte, a trailing slash or a letter in another case included, and a registered query is part of what matches. The one exception is the loopback redirection of a native application (RFC 8252 §7.3), and it belongs to a public client only: a redirect URI registered in plain HTTP on 127.0.0.1 or [::1] matches the same URI on any port, the path and the query compared byte for byte. A public client therefore registers the loopback interface by its address and never by a name: plain HTTP anywhere but on 127.0.0.1 and [::1] is refused for it (RFC 8252 §8.3). A confidential client keeps the port it registered. The same holds for post_logout_redirect_uris.

    What a registration has to hold¶

    Each rule below is checked where the client is read, so a declaration that breaks one is refused when the declarations are read, and a registration that breaks one is refused with invalid_client_metadata. A registration is refused with invalid_redirect_uri instead for a value of redirect_uris or post_logout_redirect_uris that the rule of every address refuses, and for redirect URIs its grants do not call for, or call for and lack; the rule a public client's loopback addresses follow answers invalid_client_metadata, as every other one does.

    • The client_id is printable ASCII with no space at either end (RFC 6749 appendix A.1). A client of client_credentials or of the token exchange is named where a subject is expected, the sub of its own tokens or the act of an exchanged one, so its identifier is 1 to 255 visible ASCII characters with no space at all (OIDC Core §2).
    • The client_name is not empty, and neither is any of the contacts.
    • A method that proves a shared secret has one, and any other method has none.
    • jwks and jwks_uri are never both there. One of them is, for private_key_jwt, self_signed_tls_client_auth, a request_object_signing_alg naming an algorithm, and any encrypted response. An inline jwks holds a key every encryption the client registered can be addressed to.
    • tls_client_auth comes with exactly one of the five certificate values, and every other method with none.
    • The client is registered for at least one grant. refresh_token comes with a grant that issues one: authorization_code, the device code or the backchannel grant.
    • A client of authorization_code registers at least one redirect URI, and a client of no grant that sends a browser back registers none, and no response_types either.
    • client_credentials is refused to a public client, which proves nothing.
    • The token exchange grant and the token_exchange member come together or not at all, and the token types it names are ones the provider reads.
    • requires_pkce: false is refused to a public client.
    • A client with require_signed_request_object (RFC 9101 §10.5) does not register request_object_signing_alg: none, the one value under which an unsigned request object is read.
    • backchannel_logout_session_required needs a backchannel_logout_uri, and frontchannel_logout_session_required a frontchannel_logout_uri.
    • An encryption names its alg and its enc together. OIDC Registration §2 would default the enc to A128CBC-HS256, which the provider is not wired with, so it takes no default.
    • A pairwise client has a sector: its sector_identifier_uri, its redirect URIs all on one host, or, when it registers no redirect URI, its jwks_uri.
    • Every name is one the provider is wired with: a grant, a method, an algorithm, an access token shape, a delivery mode, a kind of subject, a type of authorization detail. The refusal lists the wired names.

    Public and confidential¶

    A client is public when it registered token_endpoint_auth_method: none: it names itself and proves nothing, as a native or a browser application cannot keep a secret. Everything else is confidential. What protects the code of a public client is PKCE, which is mandatory for it, and the exact redirect URI. Besides the loopback exception above, the difference shows where a rule asks for a client that proves itself: requires_pkce: false and client_credentials are refused to a public client at its registration, the introspection endpoint refuses it, and a refresh token reaches it only when it asks for offline_access (refresh tokens).

    Reading what exists¶

    bin/console beffroi:client:list
bin/console beffroi:client:show billing
bin/console beffroi:client:delete 3x4mpl3Cl13nt1d3nt1f13rDr4wnByTh3Pr0v1d3rXX

    beffroi:client:list prints every client of the tenant with its name and its source, configuration or registration, and for a registered client when it registered and when it was last used. beffroi:client:show prints the same for one client, and for a registered one the members it registered and the ones the registration policy of the tenant wrote on it; for a declared one it points at the configuration, which is the whole of what it is. beffroi:client:delete deletes a client that registered itself, and refuses a declared one, which goes away with its block in the configuration.

    A registered client whose stored registration the reader refuses, a row naming a member this version does not read or breaking a rule it holds, is answered as no client at all, and the reason is written to the log as an error.
